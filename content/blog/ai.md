Large language models (LLMs) are perhaps the greatest invention of the modern era. I use them just about every day, for work, for home, from grocery planning, to finding blind-spots in a technical design. While I could go on about the perceived benefits of AI (as the rest of the world has done ad nauseam at this point...), I would like to rant about the drawbacks instead.

First, there's the human cost. Thousands upon thousands fired in the name of AI. I've seen it happen first-hand in Big Tech. It starts with gutting organizations, de-priortizing work, then multiple rounds of layoffs. In all fairness, before AI, post-COVID return to office was the scapegoat du jour. So it's difficult to say if AI as a technology itself is to blame. Companies are also "justifying" layoffs due to all the efficiencies gained through AI automation, or from individual workers being several times more effective with AI assistance. My cynicism says that it's primarily performative: how else can you claim that you're being part of the AI future? It's much easier to fire people than it is to show real results/profits. Short-term business thinking perpetuates this even further...

This is a death spiral in my opinion and will have real consequences when the industry hits the bottom.  1) Removing humans means removing human knowledge. I doubt most of this knowledge has been committed to AI "memory", so how do you recover it? 2) Once hiring picks up again, how much AI-generated technical debt will need to be paid down by humans? Will it have actually been worth it in the long run?

And that doesn't even cover the humanity of it all. People have lost real jobs, themselves and their families hurt. All to what end? Yet another short-term push to increase shareholder value?

The other human cost is our ability to problem-solve (and write code).  AI is essentially, "thinking as a service", and the more we offload our thinking to AI, the more we lose the ability to do it ourselves. I find this happening to myself already--when I run into a situation, my first thought is "I should ask Claude". It's the path of least resistance, and usually yields a useful answer! But now I've taken myself out of the problem-solving loop. 

Given enough time, being majorly dependent on AI for day-to-day decisions is a risk. What happens if the service is down? or I don't have my phone and I need to make an important decision? I've reinforced this belief that I can't trust myself and need to rely on AI, how am I supposed to overcome that?

This dependency has much to do with how we prompt AI. By tell it *what* to do for us, it robs us of the experience of doing it for ourselves and builds dependence. Rather, if we ask it *how* to do something, then this gives us a chance to learn principles and do the work ourselves. The issue is that prompting in this way takes considerable discipline, it's much easier to tell someone to do something for you, rather than taking the time to learn for yourself.
